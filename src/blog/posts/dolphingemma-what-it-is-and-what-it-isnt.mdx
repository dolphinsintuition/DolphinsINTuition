In April 2025, Google announced DolphinGemma — a large language model purpose-built to help scientists study dolphin communication. It was developed in partnership with Dr. Denise Herzing of the Wild Dolphin Project and Dr. Thad Starner of Google DeepMind and Georgia Tech.

Headlines followed. "Google AI decodes dolphin language." "Scientists are finally talking to dolphins."

Neither of those things is true. Here's what is.

## What DolphinGemma Actually Does

DolphinGemma is trained on decades of annotated dolphin vocalisation recordings — the Wild Dolphin Project has been collecting this data continuously since 1984. The model learns to identify acoustic units: the building blocks of dolphin communication, including signature whistles, burst-pulse sounds, and echolocation clicks.

What it does is *classification* — identifying and categorising sound types with far greater speed and consistency than a human analyst. What it doesn't do is *translation*. There is no Rosetta Stone for dolphin vocalisations. We don't know what individual sounds mean, or whether dolphin communication is compositional the way human language is (i.e., whether you can combine units to make new meaning).

That distinction matters. We hold it carefully here.

## Why It's Still Significant

The significance of DolphinGemma isn't the headline — it's the infrastructure.

Before models like this existed, acoustic analysis meant researchers listening to recordings by hand, categorising sounds one by one. A field season might yield thousands of hours of audio. Manual classification at scale was impossible. DolphinGemma changes that.

The model is deployed on Google Pixel phones in the field — researchers can now run real-time acoustic analysis during dives rather than waiting months for lab processing. That's not translation. It's compression of the time between observation and insight.

## The Wild Dolphin Project Connection

Dr. Denise Herzing has studied the same population of Atlantic spotted dolphins in the Bahamas since 1984. That's over 40 years of continuous, individual-identified observations on free-ranging wild animals — one of the longest-running cetacean studies in history.

Her team's CHAT (Cetacean Hearing and Telemetry) system has been testing two-way interaction with these dolphins since 2013, when one dolphin was documented using the system to request a toy. That's not translation either — but it is interaction. It's the beginning of something.

DolphinGemma plugs into this existing framework, giving the CHAT system a more capable acoustic classifier as its backbone.
