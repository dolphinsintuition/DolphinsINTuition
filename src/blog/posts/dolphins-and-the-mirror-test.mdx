## What Was Observed

**Shown by evidence:** In 2001, Diana Reiss and Lori Marino conducted a controlled study in which two bottlenose dolphins were exposed to mirrors and reflective surfaces. Both dolphins demonstrated what researchers call "mark-directed behavior" — they used the mirror to investigate marked parts of their own bodies. For example, when researchers applied non-toxic marks to areas the dolphins could not normally see (like their forehead or the side of their body), the dolphins swam to the mirror and oriented themselves to examine these marks. The dolphins did not treat the mirror as a social interaction with another dolphin; instead, they clearly used it as a tool for self-inspection.

## How Strong the Evidence Is

**Shown by evidence:** This behavior is extraordinarily rare in the animal kingdom. Prior to the Reiss and Marino study, only humans and great apes — chimpanzees, gorillas, and orangutans — had shown convincing evidence of mirror self-recognition. The 2001 study provided "the first convincing evidence that a nonprimate species" could recognize itself in a mirror, as the authors noted in their published research.

**Suggested but unproven:** Whether mirror self-recognition in dolphins indicates deeper forms of self-awareness — such as introspection or the ability to reason about their own mental states — remains an open question. In humans, mirror self-recognition appears around 18–24 months of age and is linked to later development of self-awareness and abstract thought. Whether dolphins possess similar capacities is not yet established.

## What Is Still Open

We do not yet know whether mirror self-recognition in dolphins reflects:
- The same neurobiological pathways that underlie it in humans and apes, or a different evolutionary solution to the same cognitive challenge
- Deeper layers of self-awareness (introspection, mental state attribution) or a narrower form of body-awareness
- A skill all dolphins possess, or one specific to certain populations or individuals with particular social histories
